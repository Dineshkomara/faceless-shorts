# Cinematic What If — Production Profile

Apply this profile only when the user or pipeline explicitly selects `cinematic-what-if`. It is an additional English style, never the default and never a replacement for Engineering Explainer, Cinematic Documentary, Deep Dive, Inside Out, Paper Atlas, Stick Story, or Tall Tale. Read `STYLE_CINEMATIC_WHAT_IF.md` before scripting or writing visual code. Keep the existing Curiography and Asakthi topic boundaries, analytics feedback, fact checking, reusable-asset library, render/cover/upload gates, and language-specific voice rules. For an `all` run, apply this profile only to the English episode; Asakthi remains in its established Telugu visual/story style.

For the selected English episode, this profile supersedes generic 3D English visuals, engineering-only topic rotation, riddle rotation, and conflicting style recipes. It does not override evidence, safe-content, factuality, audio, asset-library, or quality-gate requirements. A style selection is local to that run; never rewrite shared/default profiles or another style's files to make this one work.

The source reference is the user's pasted “What If” master prompt and the video “What If You Tasted Every Ingredient Intensely?” (https://youtu.be/Sg0aB5_CWnw). Reproduce neither its script nor specific shot sequence. Learn the production discipline and visual identity, then make original topics, characters, settings, and staging.

## A. Topic selection and evidence

Select a human-centered counterfactual: change one thing about a human sense, body, brain, or everyday ability, then push the effect to a vivid but understandable extreme. The viewer is the subject (“you/your” or universal human experience), not a different planet, a celebrity, or an invented protagonist's biography. Candidate lanes include taste, smell, hearing, memory, attention, time perception, reflexes, vision, pain, sleep, strength, metabolism, heart rate, temperature, emotion, aging, healing, lung capacity, and balance. Rotate lanes and topics so consecutive videos do not cluster on one ability.

Prefer a premise understood in under two seconds. Phrase the title in 6–8 words, in Title Case, using a “What If …?” form. Favor one of these forms: “What If You Had [extreme trait]?”, “What If [everyday experience] Felt Like [extreme]?”, or “What If Your [ability] Was [N]x [comparison]?” Use a number only when it is understandable and factual or clearly hypothetical. No jargon, horror, outrage bait, or medical-advice framing.

Before scripting, identify the premise in one sentence, the real mechanism that anchors it, its uncertainties, and whether enough credible evidence exists for multiple consequences. Verify all important claims against at least two direct, reliable sources (primary research, university/lab, government/medical authority, or reputable scientific synthesis); retain title and URL objects in `script.json`. The story can be imaginative, but never present an invented event, character, quote, study, researcher, statistic, or biological mechanism as real. Label the extreme counterfactual clearly as a thought experiment; distinguish real human baselines from extrapolation. Aim for at least five checkable facts across a full-length episode only when sources genuinely support them; do not pad a short video to hit a fact count. If evidence cannot support the premise, choose another topic.

Do not ask the user the master prompt's four setup questions during automated runs. Use configured aspect ratio, duration/format, series state, and topic override. If a required setting is absent in a manual interactive session, infer from pipeline defaults and progress; do not stall an unattended render.

## B. Narration and story arc

Use second person, present tense, warm and family-friendly cinematic delivery. Keep sentences direct and easy to say aloud. Short fragments, contrast punches, and a rhetorical question are optional tools, never quotas. Put an ellipsis only where a natural dramatic pause earns it; do not force 18–25 ellipses or 15–20 questions. Use dry humor sparingly. Spell numbers out for TTS except years; use metric. Dialogue must be brief and only when an illustrative character genuinely speaks. Do not assign speech to the narrator or animate narrator lip-sync.

Adapt the arc to runtime and fact density:

1. **Cold open:** show a vivid everyday consequence already happening, or freeze a later pivotal moment. State the odd change immediately; do not begin with a generic “Have you ever wondered?”
2. **Baseline and rule:** give a supported normal human benchmark, define the hypothetical change, and establish one limitation or rule that can drive the story. Restate the question naturally.
3. **Consequences:** escalate from a recognizable routine into sensory/body/brain consequences and then a broader emotional or social effect. Use about 6–10 short beats in a longer episode, each combining a situation, supported number or mechanism when available, result, and an occasional character reaction. Include both benefits and trade-offs.
4. **Hidden cost:** tie a cost to a real mechanism if evidence supports it; otherwise mark the consequence as speculative. Foreshadow without manufacturing danger.
5. **Human connection:** develop one relationship or community interaction when it advances the idea. Plant one small visual/story detail and pay it off later. Keep it illustrative.
6. **Crisis and choice:** in long-form only, use a comprehensible emergency or dilemma that tests the story's rule. Make physical numbers exact only when sourced; avoid gore or peril for its own sake.
7. **Resolution/payoff:** resolve the opening image, pay off the planted detail, and leave one clear takeaway. Keep the emotional tone curious and humane.
8. **Reality check:** optional and short. Identify what science actually shows, what remains unknown, and what is impossible in the premise.
9. **Sting/CTA:** use only if it fits naturally. End on a thought-provoking question or concise callback; no forced like/subscribe block.

For 30 seconds, target about 65–75 spoken words; for one minute, about 130–150; scale proportionally for longer formats. For a 1–3 minute episode, use the full arc only where it fits. Use short connected narration segments with natural punctuation and gaps; do not make the voice sound like disconnected caption fragments. If using six-second generated clip slots, target roughly 13 spoken words per slot and never exceed 15 unless that clip is deliberately longer; let voice timing determine actual shot lengths rather than squeezing narration unnaturally.

## C. Required pre-code production board

Before writing renderer code, write `review/production_board.md` with:

- Title, one-sentence premise, supported mechanism, limitation/rule, emotional payoff, duration, aspect ratio, and selected voice.
- A beat map linking every narration segment to a shot. For every shot specify: stable shot ID; time/narration segment; story purpose; set/location and time of day; all characters present with ID and locked outfit; one dominant action; opening pose/expression and exact expression/gesture changes by beat; camera shot size, angle, and move; foreground/mid/background layers and parallax; motivated key-light source and spill; optional atmosphere; exact visible graphic/text and when it appears/disappears; transition/continuity handoff; music change and any meaningful SFX cue; negative constraints for this specific shot.
- Character reference entries with complete appearance and signature colors; character-in-frame checklist matching the narration; location/color/light continuity notes; a distinct composition/shot-type tally; source-to-claim map for factual lines; and an asset-library reuse/create decision.
- Include a primary 6–8-word What If title, three alternative titles, a one-paragraph synopsis, copy-ready character reference, clip-by-clip script with word counts, the combined narration block, and one concise voice-generation direction.
- A visual negative prompt/rules checklist reused for every shot: do not alter locked design/outfit; no 3D/photoreal/anime/clay/watercolor/pixel art; no outlines; no realistic facial details; no narrator lip sync; no unscripted character; no extra text, logo, watermark, filler props, background regeneration, or static empty shot.

The board must describe the start frame and the complete in-shot movement. Each shot card is its self-contained start-frame and animation prompt: include every visible character in the start state with full locked design, environment, lighting, composition, graphics, action timeline, camera path, and negative directions. Cards should have enough concrete detail to reproduce the shot in isolation (typically 100–200 words when written as prose); structured fields are fine if they retain the information. Keep normal captions and narration in pipeline layers; no generated in-scene subtitles, unrequested spoken audio, or narrator lip-sync.

The procedural renderer's code is the production prompt: encode timing, visual staging, character expressions, background motion, camera trajectory, and graphic changes as actual deterministic animation rather than leaving any direction as vague prose. For a six-second slot, plan at least two visual events, such as camera move plus expression/diagram reveal. Useful patterns are scene-to-cutaway, zoom-and-reveal, pan-to-new-subject, and reaction-cut; use one only when it supports the beat. Do not cut to a second location just to satisfy a quota. Character action may include walk/run cycles, arm gestures, head turns/tilts, timed expression/brow/mouth change, breathing, object pick-up/hold/handoff, rim light adjusting as the character turns, or afterimage trails for genuinely extreme speed. Background motion may include drifting clouds, leaves/papers, changing lights, moving clock hands, writing, rain/wind, or a distant crowd.

## D. Shot design and graphics

Use the visual language in `STYLE_CINEMATIC_WHAT_IF.md` without weakening its locked character, lighting, environment, type, camera, and exclusion rules. Use a deliberate mix of establishing wides, medium interactions, over-shoulders, expression close-ups, inserts/macros, top-down diagrams, profiles, and tracking/pull-back reveals. Approximate the style guide's percentages over a complete episode when runtime allows; treat them as a check against monotony, not a reason to insert irrelevant angles. Plan a new composition when the story beat changes, and never put near-identical shots back-to-back.

Every shot must answer: what should the viewer look at first, what changes during this shot, and what does that change teach? Keep at most four figures in focus, usually 2–3 only when an actual interaction helps. Make the lead's facial expression and gesture readable at phone size. Use a close-up for reaction, an insert for a detail, an OTS for a relationship, a data card for a spoken number, or a blueprint for a mechanism—never use the graphic just because the style offers it. Time important numbers to narration. Keep 1–2 short text elements at most and do not restate the caption. Maintain caption and subject safe areas for the selected ratio.

Each shot should state positive direction (what exact shape, people, light, movement, event, graphic, and mood to show) and relevant negative direction (what must not appear or change). Keep the complete design original; never imitate another creator's exact compositions, characters, story wording, or thumbnail.

Implementation note: draw the visible scene with the project's shared 2D canvas context using paths, arcs, flat fills, controlled gradients, and time-based transforms. Preserve the current `OP.frame`/`OP.draw`, captions, narration timing, audio, and `tools/capture.mjs` contracts. The WebGL bootstrap may remain only if the scaffold requires it, but do not put visible `THREE` meshes, PBR surfaces, 3D cameras, or rendered 3D environments into this style. Use fixed seed/time-based motion so the same timestamp always produces the same frame. Use hand-built path silhouettes and simple reusable character/prop helpers; animate parallax, expression, camera drift, lighting spill, grain, vignette, and bars in deterministic 2D code. Keep text in the scene sparse and keep captions in the existing caption layer. For typography, bundle Fredoka Bold, Nunito Black, and Playfair Display Black Italic in the project when available to the pipeline; load them before capture. Do not silently substitute a visibly unrelated font.

## E. Sound direction

Use one consistent warm, clear young male English narrator with natural connected speech and no forced character ensemble. Delivery is conversational, intimate, slightly awed, cinematic, and moderate-to-slightly-fast with clear pronunciation; let emotional lines breathe more slowly, emphasize contrast and important numbers, and never sound robotic, shouty, or overdramatic. Natural pauses are allowed; do not force ellipsis counts. Build a warm, restrained, curious bed that evolves through discovery and resolves at the payoff. Use only a few soft, scene-matched sounds at genuinely audible actions: a gentle bloom/reveal, quiet utensil/food details, subtle movement, a held breath, or a purposeful pop/tock. Do not add a riser or whoosh to every cut, stack effects under important syllables, or use generic dramatic booms. Every cue in `data/cues.json` must map to a real implemented cue in `tools/mix.py`. Duck music smoothly, preserve breaths and consonants, and inspect the full mix. Measure integrated loudness and true peak (target near −14 LUFS and at or below −1 dBTP after AAC) and listen for masking, pumping, clipping, harsh sibilance, repeated motifs, or dead air.

## F. Required deliverables and release gate

Produce the pipeline's actual video package—not a text-only response:

1. `script.json` with title, premise, complete narration segments, sources, duration/style/aspect metadata, and the configured music choice.
2. `review/production_board.md` as specified above.
3. Locked character references/manifest and useful original procedural props/sets; search the reusable asset library first and archive only modular assets worth reusing.
4. Narration, word timing/captions, cue manifest, and final ducked mix; all captions legible and not burned twice.
5. Source project code and rendered video at the configured aspect/duration, a matching clean cover, a full contact sheet, and at least five full-size reviewed frames from distinct beats/sets (including opening, main character interaction, close detail, mechanism/data, payoff, and ending as available). The cover must use this style's dark-ink lead, exact blue signature jacket and cream/amber rim edge, a clear premise-driven pose or visual contradiction, and a short high-contrast title phrase integrated into the scene. Keep the cover readable at thumbnail size; no unrelated stock imagery, wrong outfit, watermark, or duplicate paragraph text.
6. Completed `review/quality_review.json` and feedback-loop QA. All shared strict quality-gate scores/checks remain binding. Do not claim an audio listen, source check, frame review, render, or upload that was not actually completed.

Fix character drift, weak silhouette, unreadable face/graphics, duplicated text, repeated compositions, visual noise, poor parallax, static shots, factual errors, narration timing, and audio problems before staging. A failed quality gate means fix and review again; do not stage or upload a failed candidate.
